FocalCharacter: Mara Voss

The harbor patrol office kept its own kind of tide. Radio traffic rose and fell with the night the way water moved against the pilings outside, and by eleven o'clock Mara Voss had learned to read the quiet between calls the same way she'd once read current under a dive light — patient, half-blind, trusting her hands more than her eyes.

She'd walked the dock before her shift started, the way she did most nights, down past the fuel pumps and the ice house to where the working boats tied up two and three deep. Low tide had already begun its slow retreat by then, the pilings showing their dark waterlines like rings on a tree, and the smell had come up off the exposed mud in that particular Amity Cove way — brine and rot and something almost sweet underneath it, a smell she'd grown up thinking was ordinary and only understood, years later and a hundred miles from anywhere with a harbor, was not.

Colm Reyes had been out on the bait shop's back step, restocking the cooler by the light of a bare bulb, when she passed.

"You're early," he said, not looking up from the tray of squid he was sorting.

"You're always here. I don't know how that's a metric."

"It's the only metric that matters." He straightened, wiped his hands on his apron, and squinted past her at the water, the way he did — Colm had spent so many years reading tide charts for the shop that he seemed to feel changes in the harbor the way other people felt weather in a joint. "Tide's wrong tonight."

"Tide's early. Dez already gave me the whole speech."

"Not early. Wrong." He said it plainly, without drama, which was somehow more unsettling than if he'd made a show of it. Colm didn't dramatize things; he'd been the calm one since they were kids racing each other off the town dock, the one who pulled her back when she wanted to swim past the buoy line just to see how far out she could get before someone yelled. "Chart says low's not till one-forty. Water's already down like it's three in the morning. It did that Tuesday too."

"Tuesday you also told me the crabs were running wrong."

"Crabs are always running wrong. This is different." He shrugged, but his eyes stayed on the water a beat longer than the shrug suggested. "You'll think I'm an old man talking to himself."

"You're thirty-six."

"I feel like an old man talking to himself." He grinned then, quick, the expression that had gotten him out of more trouble than his sense had ever gotten him into, and tossed her a strip of dried bait like he used to when they were fifteen and she still had a boat under her that wasn't the patrol's. "Go do your radio thing. Tell Dez I said the tide's wrong."

"He'll say the tide's early."

"He'll say that. He'll be wrong too."

She'd laughed, taken the bait strip out of habit more than hunger, and walked the rest of the way to the office with the smell of exposed mud flat following her the whole distance, thinking about nothing in particular, which was its own kind of luxury she wouldn't recognize as one until much later.

Now she sat with her boots on the desk edge and the radio volume low enough to catch a hail but not so low she'd miss the click of someone keying up and thinking better of it. Dez Okafor said that click meant more than half the calls that followed it. People who hesitated before they spoke were usually the ones telling the truth.

"You're doing the thing again," Dez said from the doorway, not looking up from the thermos he was topping off with coffee that had gone past strong into something closer to tar.

"What thing."

"Listening to nothing like it owes you money."

"It's a slow channel tonight."

"It's always a slow channel until it isn't." He came in, dropped into the chair across from her that had been his chair for as long as she'd worked dispatch, and which nobody else bothered sitting in even when he wasn't there. Twenty-six years running the harbor patrol had given Dez a particular kind of furniture gravity. "Low tide's early. Big drop. You feel it?"

"Colm says it's not early. Says it's wrong. Different thing, according to him."

Dez's mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Colm's been saying the tide's wrong since he was nineteen. Kid reads more into a chart than the chart's got in it."

"He's not usually wrong, though."

"Didn't say he was wrong. Said he reads into it." Dez drank his coffee and looked out the window at the black water and the string of dock lights that marked out the marina in dots, like something spelled out for a plane overhead. Beyond them, past the last public dock, floodlights burned white and steady over a stretch of waterfront that used to belong to Iversen's boatyard before Iversen sold it. Ridge Coastal Holdings had put up a construction fence with a rendering on it — clean lines, glass, a walkway lined with young trees that didn't exist yet anywhere in Amity Cove — and the floodlights ran all night so nobody could say the site wasn't being watched.

"Ridge's people were out there again this evening," Dez said. "Surveying past the tide line. I told them the harbor patrol doesn't babysit contractors after dark, and the fella just smiled at me. Good teeth. Expensive jacket."

"That's Thomas Ridge's whole personality," Mara said. "Good teeth and an expensive jacket."

"You'd know. Your sister works for him."

It wasn't said unkindly, which was somehow worse. Mara kept her eyes on the radio and didn't answer right away, and Dez, to his credit, let it sit instead of pushing. That was the shape of their working relationship — twelve years of it, going back to when she'd washed out of harbor diving with a knee that wouldn't hold a fin kick anymore and Dez had put her on the radio instead of letting the town's only trained diver walk away from the water entirely. He didn't talk about that either, not directly. He just kept her close enough to the harbor that she couldn't pretend it wasn't still hers.

"Lena does site logistics," Mara said finally. "Trucks and permits. It's not glamorous."

"Didn't say it was."

"You were thinking it."

"I think a lot of things I don't say. Occupational hazard of the job." He set the thermos down and reached for the clipboard hanging by the window, more habit than need — the boat log for the night was three lines long, nothing past a couple of lobstermen checking pots and the ferry's last run. "Lena still not talking to you?"

"Lena's fine. Lena's employed and has dental and thinks I made a choice four years ago that I didn't actually get to make."

"About your dad."

"About my dad." Mara said it flat, the way you said a fact you'd worn smooth from handling. Their father had died on a boat that wasn't his, doing a favor for a friend, on water Mara had grown up diving and Lena had grown up hating. Mara had stayed. Lena had taken the first job that paid her to look at the harbor as an asset line instead of a place. Neither of them had been able to forgive the other for the version of grief they'd chosen. It had been four years, and the space between the Voss sisters had only gotten more precisely shaped over that time, like something worn into a doorframe by a door that closes the same way every night.

Dez didn't push further. He never did, not on that. He drank his coffee and let the radio hiss its low static, and outside the window the construction floodlights burned their patient white light over the mud flats, throwing the fence's shadow bars long and even across ground that had been underwater six hours before.

"You ever think about what's under there," Mara said, mostly to change the subject, nodding toward the site. "Before they pour anything."

"Boat bones, mostly. Iversen scuttled two hulls out past the old breakwater instead of paying to haul them off. Common enough, back when nobody was looking." Dez shrugged. "Ridge's surveyors will find worse than that eventually. Everybody's got something on the bottom of a harbor they'd rather not explain."

"That supposed to mean something?"

"Means I've been doing this a long time, and low tide always shows you more than you asked to see." He said it lightly, but something moved behind his eyes when he said it, something that didn't match the lightness, and Mara filed it the way she filed everything Dez said and didn't explain — for later, for when it made sense on its own. It was a habit she'd built over twelve years of working under him: store the odd thing, wait for the pattern.

She thought, not for the first time, that she'd inherited that particular patience from her father, who'd been the kind of diver who could hang motionless in cold water for a full minute just watching before he moved, and that Lena had inherited none of it, which was maybe the real difference between them and not the job, not the money, not any of the things they said out loud when they fought.

The radio cracked.

It wasn't a full transmission, just the carrier tone of someone keying up and holding it a half-second before letting go, the exact hesitation Dez had been talking about twenty minutes earlier. Mara's boots came off the desk before she'd decided to move them.

"Dispatch, this is—" The voice broke up, came back thinner. "—checking in, we're maybe a half mile past the second buoy, everything's—"

Static ate the rest of it.

Mara had the mic in her hand already. "Vessel calling dispatch, say again, you're broken up. Identify yourself and your position."

Nothing came back but the hiss of an open channel with no one on the other end of it, and under that, so faint she almost decided she'd imagined it, something that might have been a second voice, or might have been water moving against a hull, or might have been nothing at all.

Dez was on his feet now, the coffee forgotten, his whole posture gone taut in a way Mara had seen maybe four times in twelve years working under him. "Who's out past the second buoy tonight?"

Mara was already flipping back through the log, three thin lines of a slow night suddenly not thin enough. "Nobody logged. Nobody's supposed to be out there — not at low tide, not with that draft." She kept the mic close, thumb on the switch. "Vessel calling dispatch, repeat your last, we are not receiving your position."

The channel gave her nothing. Not silence, exactly — the ordinary electrical hiss of an open line, patient as the tide, waiting to see what she'd do with it.

Outside, past the marina lights and past the fence with its rendering of a harbor that didn't exist yet, the mud flats lay open and gleaming under the floodlights, low tide holding the whole cove exposed and waiting, the way it always did before it decided to come back in. Somewhere past the second buoy, on water that Colm had said was behaving wrong two nights running, something had tried to say its name and hadn't managed to finish.
