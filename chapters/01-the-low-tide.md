FocalCharacter: Mara Voss

Mara Voss had learned to tell the hour by the smell of the harbor before she learned to tell it by the clock. At low tide, Averlin smelled of iron and old rope, of mudflats breathing out everything the sea had swallowed and reconsidered. At high tide, it smelled almost like a normal town: salt, diesel, the yeasty warmth of the bakery on Cannery Row. This morning it smelled low, though the almanac in her coat pocket swore the water should have already turned.

She stood on the seawall with the almanac open against her knee, the pages soft as cloth from a decade of thumbing, and did the arithmetic in her head twice because the number refused to sit still the first time. High tide, seven forty-two. It was eight fifteen. The water below her had the flat, exposed look of an hour past dead low, gray flats stretching out where a channel should have been, gulls stalking through puddles that shouldn't exist yet.

She wrote 8:15 — flats exposed, no turn in the margin, in the small neat hand she used for nothing else but this ledger, and told herself it was an instrument error before she told herself anything more interesting.

Averlin kept its records the way some families kept grudges: carefully, and for much longer than made sense. The town had drowned twice in its recorded history — once in a hurricane in 1911 that nobody living remembered except through photographs, and once in a slower, more embarrassing way, through decades of the harbor silting and the fishing boats going elsewhere. What was left was a town that measured itself against the water obsessively, the way a person recovering from an illness keeps checking their own pulse. Mara was the town's official keeper of that pulse. Tide clerk, the deed to the job said, though nobody had called it that since her grandmother held it. Everyone in Averlin just called her Mara, or, if they wanted something, Miss Voss.

She was thirty-four, dark-haired going gray at one temple in a streak she'd stopped dyeing out two winters ago, tall enough that she'd spent her adolescence trying to fold herself smaller in doorways. She had her mother's hands — blunt, capable, quick with a knot — and her grandmother's patience for numbers that didn't add up, which was, she sometimes thought, the family's actual inheritance, more than the house or the ledgers or the brass tide gauge bolted to the seawall like something that expected to outlive everyone who read it.

The gauge was reading wrong too. She crouched to look at it properly, brushing gull droppings off the glass with her sleeve, and the needle sat exactly where the almanac said it shouldn't: low, and by the look of the barnacle line on the piling beside it, low for longer than one bad morning could explain. The barnacles told their own kind of time, a white ring marking the tide's honest average over weeks, and today's water sat a good foot below where that ring said it belonged.

"You're going to fall in doing that one day," said a voice behind her, and she didn't have to turn around to know it was Theo.

Theo Kade had the gift of narrating her more embarrassing habits back to her as though they were charming, a gift he'd been cultivating since they were nine years old and he'd talked her into climbing the fire escape of the old cannery to see if the roof really did have a view of three counties. It did not. He'd fallen learning that. She still had the scar on her palm from where she'd caught him.

"I'm not going to fall in," she said. "I've been doing this since I was nineteen."

"You've been doing this since you were nineteen and you fell in twice." He came to stand beside her at the rail, a coffee in each hand, one of which he passed her without being asked. He smelled like the boatyard — diesel and fiberglass resin — because he'd been down at his uncle's shop since six, patching the hull of somebody's summer sailboat before the season made everyone frantic about their sailboats at once. He looked at the flats the way she'd been looking at them, and some of the ease went out of his shoulders. "That's not right."

"No."

"Storm somewhere pulling the water off?"

"Nothing on the marine forecast." She'd checked twice, on the drive in and again standing here, thumb scrolling the weather service page until the signal dropped, the way it always did on the seawall. "Nothing anywhere, actually. Clear for four hundred miles."

Theo drank his coffee and studied the flats with the specific squint he used for engines that wouldn't turn over, a look that assumed the problem was mechanical and findable if you were patient enough to trace the wiring. Mara loved him for that squint and had loved him, at various unhelpful points in her life, for reasons that had nothing to do with tides, though she'd long since filed those feelings under things it was easier not to reopen. He'd married and unmarried since then; she'd left for the state university and come back with a degree that qualified her for exactly the job she'd have gotten anyway. They'd settled, without ever discussing it, into something that functioned like family — closer than friendship, careful in the specific way people are careful with something they've already broken once and repaired well enough that they don't want to test the seams.

"Could be the gauge," he said. "Corrosion in the float chamber, something sticking."

"Could be." She didn't believe it, and from the way he said it, neither did he; it was the kind of thing you offered so the other person had something reasonable to hold onto before you both admitted you didn't have an explanation. "I'll check the mechanism. But the barnacle line's wrong too, Theo. That's not the gauge lying. That's weeks of actual water being somewhere else."

"Somewhere else." He said it like a place you could look up. "You want to walk out on the flats? See if there's a channel at all, or if it just — stopped."

She considered it, the practical part of her brain already listing objections — the mud out past the third groyne could swallow a boot to the ankle in the height of summer, and there was no reason today would be gentler — while another part of her, the part that had filled forty pages of a ledger in a careful hand looking for exactly one page that didn't match, was already stepping down off the seawall.

"Boots," she said. "Real ones. And you're not falling in either."

The flats held smells she associated with disaster movies and grade-school field trips: sulfur, decay, brine gone stagnant in the sun before the tide could refresh it. They picked their way out past the pilings that marked where the working docks had been before the harbor moved, Theo testing the ground ahead of her with the flat of a boot the way he'd have tested ice, and the mud held firmer than she expected, packed down as though the water had left it slowly rather than in a rush.

It was Theo who found the channel, or what was left of it — a groove in the flats maybe six feet across, running out toward open water, its bottom cracked into plates like a dry lakebed, no more than an inch of standing water pooled in the deepest cracks. He crouched at its edge and touched one of the plates and it didn't crumble the way drying mud should; it held together, smooth, almost polished, as though something had pressed it flat rather than let it dry on its own.

"Mara." He didn't look up. "How long does mud take to crack like this?"

She didn't know, not with any precision, but she knew it wasn't hours. She thought of the ledger in her coat pocket, of forty years of her grandmother's handwriting before her own, every page agreeing with the one before it in a way that had made the record feel less like data and more like a promise the town had made to itself: the water comes in, the water goes out, and Averlin, silted and diminished as it was, could at least count on the arithmetic of that.

She crouched beside Theo and looked down the channel toward the horizon, where the gray flats gave way at last to gray water, further out than she had ever seen it stand still, and further out, she understood with a small cold drop in her stomach that had nothing to do with the wind, than any tide had a right to pull it.

"I don't know," she said. "But I'm going to find out."

She took out the ledger, found the next clean line beneath 8:15 — flats exposed, no turn, and wrote the time again, because whatever this was, it deserved its own honest entry, the way everything else in that book had earned one before it. Behind her, Theo was already photographing the cracked plates with his phone, narrating findings to no one the way he did when he was nervous and trying not to show it, and above them both the gulls had gone quiet in a way she would only think to notice later, once she understood there had been nothing left out there for them to want.
